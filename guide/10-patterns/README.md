# The patterns

Six chapters on what the course website was doing during class, and how to run
each of those things without it. Five are classroom procedures. The sixth is
mostly a decision about third-party tools, with a short procedure attached.

## What is evidence here, and what is not

These chapters mix four kinds of claim, and it is worth knowing which is which
before you plan a class around one.

**Observed.** What the software did, and what happened when it did. The course
ran on it for a full semester, so this is the part with a room behind it: what
each component collected, what the AI was instructed to prize, what the
instructor could and could not see, and where it disappointed. Every chapter's
"what the software was doing" and "what you lose" sections are this kind of
claim, and they are the most reliable paragraphs in the guide.

**Designed.** The paper procedures themselves. **None of them has been run with
students.** The software did every one of these jobs all semester, so what you
have is the same pedagogical work translated for cards and a whiteboard by
someone who watched the machine do it and formed strong opinions about what it
was for. Translations from a working original are a reasonable starting point.
They are not tested practice, and the first person to run one will be you.

**Predicted.** The "what it should gain you" sections, and the "what you gain"
section of the External Tools chapter. These say what a procedure is designed to
buy you, and since none has been run, none of it has been seen. They are the most
confident writing in these chapters and the least evidenced, which is why they
are labelled where they appear.

**Estimated.** Every number attached to a paper procedure. Four seconds to grade
a ticket at the door, ninety seconds per artifact on the projector when a room
cannot run a gallery walk, four to five
minutes per collection point, five minutes for the pairing step. These are
guesses by someone who knows the room. None was timed, because there was nothing
to time: the software was doing the job.

That distinction is not a technicality. The pacing chapter holds the course's own
timing data to a standard that discards twenty-four of twenty-six recorded
stages. Held to that standard, every number in these six chapters would be
discarded too, because no instrument was ever pointed at them. Treat them as
budgeting hints, notice what they actually cost you, and trust your own numbers
over these the moment you have any.

## Why the software version is still worth reading

If none of the procedures is tested, the obvious question is what the chapters
are for.

The answer is that the design problem is the transferable part. Each chapter
names a job the software was doing that a class still needs done — collecting
what everyone thinks and getting the disagreements in front of the room,
verifying that the reading happened, keeping one student from running a
discussion — and the software version is a worked example of doing that job,
with a semester of watching it behind the description. The paper procedure is one
answer. Your room may want a different one, and the chapter is written so you can
build it: what the job is, what the machine was doing about it, and what gets
lost when the machine is not there.

Read the "what you lose" sections closely. They are the honest part, and they are
where the semester actually shows.
