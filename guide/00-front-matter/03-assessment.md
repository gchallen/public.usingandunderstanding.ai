title: Assessment

Most adopters have to put a number in a gradebook, and the rest of this kit is
about what happens in the room rather than what happens afterward. This chapter
is what the original did, why, and where it is weakest — which is the part a
future instructor is most likely to improve on.

## What the original did

Fifty percent of the grade was participation in labs and discussions. Quizzes
were thirty-five, the final project ten, and a final video five. Attendance was
expected with three no-questions-asked absences.

That fifty is the part that matters here, and it was participation in a specific
sense rather than a soft one. Lab meetings had an activity to complete for
credit, and the completion was the artifact: the thing you did in the room, seen
by the instructor while you did it. Discussion meetings required the pre-class
conversation and then showing up and talking. Nothing was collected afterward
and graded at home.

Two things made that workable, and both are worth stating plainly because they
are exactly what an adopter may not have.

**Eleven students.** The instructor could see the whole room. Whether someone
participated was not an inference from an artifact; it was something observed
directly, every session, for a whole semester. At thirty students that
observation gets thinner. At sixty it is gone, and participation grading becomes
either a proxy for attendance or a fiction.

**Software that watched.** The website recorded who submitted what, who reached
ready on a preparation conversation, and who spoke in a group chat. The
instructor was not tracking eleven people from memory. Take that away, which
this kit does, and you are tracking it yourself or you are not tracking it.

## Why participation rather than products

The course is about judgment, and judgment shows up in argument rather than in
artifacts. A student who changes their mind mid-discussion has demonstrated the
thing the course is for, and there is no submission box that captures it. The
grading scheme followed the pedagogy: the disagreement patterns run through the
whole guide, and grading collected essays would have quietly told students that
the essay was the point.

It also removes the obvious cheating question. When the assessed thing is what
you said in a room, whether you used AI to prepare is not a problem to police.

## Where this is weakest

**It does not scale, and the kit does not tell you how to make it.** Everything
above rests on a room of eleven with software watching it. There is no version
here for thirty students on paper, and pretending otherwise would be inventing
practice rather than reporting it.

**It is vulnerable to the quiet student.** Participation grading rewards
talking, and reticence is not the same as disengagement. The original mitigated
this with the pair structure — most discussion happened in twos, where staying
silent is harder and less exposed than in a full room — but mitigating is not
solving.

**Nothing here was validated.** No inter-rater reliability, no comparison
against another scheme, no evidence the fifty percent measured what it claimed.
It is one instructor's judgment about eleven students he saw twice a week.

## If you need a graded artifact per session

Many departments require one, and the paper procedures produce collectable
things almost by accident. Three that the guide already generates:

- **The reading ticket** is individual by design, handed in at the door, and
  graded check / check-plus / no credit in seconds. The Reading Ticket chapter
  is emphatic that it must not become homework you take away, and that
  constraint is what keeps it cheap enough to actually do.
- **The card from a Card Sort** carries a name and one idea. It is evidence
  someone thought about the question before the room discussed it.
- **The writing space on the handout** is collectable, but be careful: most of
  it is filled in during pair work, so two names on one page is a free-rider
  problem the Reading Ticket chapter warns about at length.

What none of these gives you is a defensible mark for the discussion itself.

## An invitation

This is the thinnest chapter in the guide and it is the one most likely to be
improved by somebody else. If you teach a version of this course and work out a
participation scheme that holds up at thirty or sixty students, or a rubric for
discussion that a department will accept, that is genuinely new work rather than
an adaptation of ours, and we would like to hear about it.
