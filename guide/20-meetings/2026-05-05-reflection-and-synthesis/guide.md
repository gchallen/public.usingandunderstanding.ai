## Facilitation overview

## Facilitation Notes

**Philosophy:** This is the closing meeting. Apr 28 already did the deep argument-engagement on AGI / flourishing using Sun, Shroff, and Pollan. Doing that again would feel like deja vu. Instead, Round 1 is a personal retrospective — what shifted in students over the semester, and how they will live alongside AI from here. Light, not exhaustive.

The real weight of this meeting is on Rounds 2 and 3: the future of the course itself. Students are the only people in the room who actually lived through this version. The agent-guided group chats capture their feedback in a recoverable form so it can shape next year's design.

There is no `feedback` block at the end. The whole meeting is feedback. The Round 3 share-out is the close.

**Logistics:**
- Three discussion rounds with new partners each round (same `groupKey` for partner rotation)
- Each round: 10-minute discussion followed by a 10-minute share-out
- Round 1 captures key points via text-submission (no group chat — keeps it light)
- Rounds 2 and 3 use agent-guided group chats for capture
- Share-outs are instructor-led: ask 2-3 pairs to share, then bridge to the next round

**Pacing:**
| Stage | Duration | Cumulative |
|-------|----------|------------|
| Round 1: What Changed in You? | 10m | 10m |
| Round 1: Share Out | 10m | 20m |
| Round 2: What This Course Got Right | 10m | 30m |
| Round 2: Share Out | 10m | 40m |
| Round 3: What Should Change | 10m | 50m |
| Round 3: Share Out + Closing | 10m | 60m |

(60m of content fits the 70m Tuesday window with buffer.)

**Tips:**
- Round 1 is intentionally personal and low-stakes. Apr 28's discussion gave students sharper tools (Sun, Shroff, Pollan). Today's question is what they do with those tools, not whether the arguments are right. Push past abstract answers — "AI will change everything" is not a useful answer for *you*. The text-submission gives you something to read after.
- Round 2 (what worked) needs probing. Generic praise is the failure mode. The group chat is configured to push past "the discussions were good" toward the bet behind the design — *why* it worked, not just *what* worked. Reinforce this in the share-out: ask pairs not just what they liked but what bet was paying off underneath.
- Round 3 (what to change) is the most useful round for next year. Push students past surface complaints toward structural suggestions, and especially toward what was *missing entirely*. The group chat probes for this directly. A student saying "the readings were too long" is less useful than "we read three articles per discussion when we only had time to engage with one — pair one reading with one sharp question."
- Closing remarks: this is the last gathered conversation of the semester. The Apr 28 meeting closed with "you should be skeptical of anyone who tells you they know either way" and a forward look. Today's close should land the semester. A few moves that work:
  - Thank them. They built things, they read articles that were sometimes warmer than they appeared, they had real conversations with each other and with AI about questions that matter.
  - Name the bet the course represents: that learning to use AI critically is more valuable than either celebrating it or refusing to engage. They are leaving with sharper tools than they came in with.
  - Hand off. Thursday is the project showcase. The video reflections close the loop personally. After that, they go out into a workforce shaped by what they have just spent fourteen weeks examining.
- Watch for: students who want to keep talking after Round 3 ends. Let them. The 10-minute share-out is a soft cap, not a hard one.

### Stage 1: Round 1: What Changed in You?

**Timing:** planned 10m

_Partners discuss what shifted over the semester_

> **Facilitation notes**
>
> Personal retrospective. Apr 28 gave students sharper tools for thinking about AGI and flourishing using Sun (specificity of a life), Shroff (who pays for the bubble), and Pollan (consciousness gap). Today's question is what they actually *do* with those tools, not whether the arguments are right.
> 
> Push past abstract answers. "AI will change everything" is not a useful answer for *you*. The interesting answers are concrete: a belief that shifted, a habit that changed, a rule a student is going to set for themselves about when to reach for AI and when not to.
> 
> The text-submission captures something durable from each pair. Read these after class — they tell you what fourteen weeks of this course actually moved.

**Grouping:** 2 per group

#### What Changed in You?

You walked into this room in January with some set of assumptions about AI. Maybe you thought the hype was overblown, or that AGI was around the corner, or that ChatGPT was magic, or that none of it really mattered for your major. Fourteen weeks later, you have used these tools weekly in labs, read journalism that took the technology seriously and skeptically, and learned the actual mechanisms — next-token prediction, RLHF, embeddings, scaling, mixture of experts — that produce the outputs you see on the screen.

Last Tuesday we sat with three sharp arguments: Sun on the specificity of a life, Shroff on who pays when the bubble bursts, Pollan on the consciousness gap underneath the AGI promise. You do not have to have settled any of them.

**Discuss with your partner.** Compared to who you were in January, what do you believe about AI now that you didn't then? What is one belief that shifted — toward more skepticism, more enthusiasm, more nuance, more confusion?

Then go forward, not backward. **What does flourishing alongside AI actually look like for you?** Be specific to your life: when will you reach for these tools, and when won't you? What rule are you going to set for yourself? What does using AI well — for your goals, your work, your relationships — actually mean now that you know how it works?

**Capture your key points:**

_Expected response: What shifted in your thinking this semester, and how do you plan to live alongside AI from here?_

### Stage 3: Round 2: What This Course Got Right

**Timing:** planned 10m

_New partners discuss which bets the course made paid off_

> **Facilitation notes**
>
> The course was built on specific bets: no CS prerequisites, AI examined critically rather than celebrated, paired discussions over lectures, hands-on labs, AI woven into the course mechanics (preparation chats, group chats), Atlantic-and-podcast readings, mandatory feedback. Round 2 asks which of those bets paid off.
> 
> The failure mode is generic praise — "the discussions were good." The group chat is configured to push past that toward the bet behind the design. *What* worked is the surface; *why* it worked is the useful answer for next year. Reinforce this in the share-out.
> 
> Probe in the share-out for things that were good but invisible. Some of the design choices (preparation chats before discussions, three-round rotation with new partners, the course site itself being human-AI collaboration) were quiet. Students may have appreciated them without noticing.

**Grouping:** 2 per group

#### What This Course Got Right

This course was built on a set of bets. That you do not need a CS background to think clearly about AI. That AI should be examined critically, not celebrated. That conversations work better than lectures. That you learn AI by using it (creative media, study guides, data analysis, websites, your final project). That AI itself can be part of the course — preparation chats before discussions, agent-guided synthesis during them, a course site that is itself human-AI collaboration.

Some of those bets paid off. Some did not. You are the only people in the room who can say which.

**Discuss with your partner.** Which bets paid off for you? Push past "I liked the discussions." Which discussion? What did it do that a lecture wouldn't have? Was there a lab where you noticed yourself learning something you couldn't have learned by reading? A reading that hit harder than expected? A moment where the agent-guided format actually changed what you said? Be specific.

Then push to the bet underneath: *what about the design* made it work? That is the part that needs to survive into next year.

### Stage 5: Round 3: What Should Change

**Timing:** planned 10m

_New partners redesign the course for next year_

> **Facilitation notes**
>
> The most useful round for next year. The frame is "you are the next instructor designing this course." Push past surface complaints toward structural suggestions and toward what is *missing entirely*.
> 
> Generic complaints ("less reading") are less useful than structural ones ("we read three articles per discussion when we only had bandwidth for one — pair one reading with one sharp question"). The group chat probes for this directly.
> 
> Listen especially for what is missing entirely. The course did not cover: anything about open-source models, almost nothing about image and video generation models, almost nothing about education and AI specifically, no formal treatment of cost or compute economics, only glancing treatment of agentic systems. Students may surface these or others. They are also the people who can name what was missing that you wouldn't think of.
> 
> Do not defend the course in the share-out. The students are the experts here. Your job is to gather clear feedback, not to argue.

**Grouping:** 2 per group

#### What Should Change

You are the next instructor designing this course. You have to make it better than the version you just took. What do you do?

**Discuss with your partner.** What do you cut? What do you add? What is in the course but in the wrong shape — the right idea executed badly, or the wrong amount, or the wrong timing? What is *missing entirely* — a topic the course never touched, a format you never got to try, an experience you wished you had?

Push past surface complaints. "Less reading" is a complaint; "fewer readings paired more tightly with one sharp question per meeting" is a redesign. "More labs" is a wish; "an extra lab on X, cutting Y to make room" is a trade. The next instructor needs trades, not wishes.

The hardest and most useful question: **what did the course never talk about that it should have?**
