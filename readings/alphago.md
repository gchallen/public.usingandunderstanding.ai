# AlphaGo (Documentary)

AlphaGo - The Movie · 2017-04-21

<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WXuK6gekU1Y>

The article itself is not included here. These are the course's annotations,
each quoting the phrase it was attached to.

## 1. "It found the optimal strategy was to dig a tunnel around the side, and put the ball round the back of the wall."

The tunnel strategy illustrates emergent behavior — the system wasn't programmed to dig tunnels, it discovered this through reinforcement learning. This is the kind of result that makes AI researchers excited and nervous in equal measure. The agent found a solution its creators didn't know about.

## 2. "the number of possible configurations of the board is more than the number of atoms in the universe"

Chess has roughly 10^47 possible games. Go has roughly 10^170. The observable universe has about 10^80 atoms. Brute-force search — the strategy that worked for chess — is mathematically impossible for Go. This is why Go required a fundamentally different AI approach: learning intuition rather than exhaustively calculating.

## 3. "I lose with a program. And I don't understand myself anymore."

The emotional response here isn't just competitive disappointment — it's closer to an identity crisis. Fan Hui has spent his life understanding the world through Go, and something he can't read or relate to has beaten him. This theme — AI unsettling not just our abilities but our sense of self — recurs throughout the documentary.

## 4. "AlphaGo said there was a 1-in-10,000 probability that move 37 would have been played by a human player. So it knew that this was an extremely unlikely move."

AlphaGo's policy network — trained on human games — said there was only a 0.01% chance a human would play this move. But its value network — which evaluates board positions — determined it was strong. The system overrode human conventional wisdom with its own evaluation. This is what people mean by AI "creativity": not consciousness or intention, but finding solutions outside the space humans typically explore.

## 5. "I already saw the commentators call AlphaGo like "he" and "she" during the games. Completely unconsciously."

Pay attention to how the documentary itself does this — the commentators, the Go professionals, even the DeepMind researchers slip into treating AlphaGo as a person. Julian calls it "a very, very simple program," yet humans can't help projecting agency and personality onto it. This connects directly to our course discussions about anthropomorphization of AI systems.

## 6. "AlphaGo look like the real mirror. When you play with AlphaGo, you feel very strange. You look like you're all the time naked."

Fan Hui keeps returning to this idea — that playing AlphaGo is like looking in a mirror, being "naked." Because AlphaGo has no personality, no style, no emotions to read, players are left entirely alone with their own thinking. Every weakness is exposed without the social dynamics that normally shape a competitive game. This is a profound observation about what AI can reveal about us.

## 7. "What probability does it give it as white? 0.007%."

The mirror image of move 37. AlphaGo's policy network gave this move a 0.007% probability — even lower than move 37. Lee Sedol said it was "the only move" he could see. Where move 37 showed AI finding what humans couldn't, move 78 showed a human finding what AI couldn't. Together they suggest human and machine cognition have different blind spots.

## 8. "A "slack move" is a move that looks lazy. You can see these other better moves, and AlphaGo is rejecting them."

This reveals something fundamental about how AlphaGo evaluates positions. It maximizes probability of winning, not margin of victory. Once ahead, it plays seemingly lazy moves that reduce its margin but also reduce risk. Humans found this baffling — it looked like AlphaGo was toying with its opponent. But it's rational: why take risks when you're already winning? The lesson for Go players was that humans had been confusing "winning by a lot" with "winning safely."
