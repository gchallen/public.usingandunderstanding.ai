## 1. "the greatest degree of compression can be achieved by understanding the text."

This specific passage becomes a pivot point in the debate about AI understanding. In his 2025 New Yorker article "The Case That A.I. Is Thinking," James Somers quotes this line to argue *for* AI understanding — if LLMs achieve extreme compression, and compression requires understanding, then perhaps LLMs actually understand. Chiang's own metaphor, turned on its head.

## 2. "But I'm going to make a prediction: when assembling the vast amount of text used to train GPT-4, the people at OpenAI will have made every effort to exclude material generated by ChatGPT or any other large language model."

Chiang was largely correct. By 2024, "model collapse" — the degradation that occurs when AI models train on AI-generated text — had become a [major research concern](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y). Companies now actively filter synthetic text from training data, exactly as Chiang predicted.

## 3. "Your first draft isn't an unoriginal idea expressed clearly; it's an original idea expressed poorly"

This argument connects directly to the course's central question about understanding. If writing is a form of thinking — if the struggle to express an idea *is* the process of understanding it — then using AI to skip that struggle means skipping the understanding itself. The same compression/understanding link Chiang builds earlier applies: you can't compress your thoughts into clear prose without first understanding them.
