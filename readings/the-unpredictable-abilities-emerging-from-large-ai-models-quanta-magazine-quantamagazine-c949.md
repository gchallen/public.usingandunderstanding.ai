## 1. "predict what comes next"

Tuesday's digit network had one job too: take pixel values in, output a digit classification. Nobody programmed it to recognize the *style* of a "7" or to handle messy handwriting — those abilities emerged from training. The same principle applies here, but at a scale millions of times larger.

## 2. "parameters"

"Parameters" are the same thing as the weights you adjusted in Tuesday's digit network — numbers that determine how strongly one piece of information influences another. The digit network had about 13,000 of these. GPT-3 has 175 billion. GPT-4 likely has over a trillion. Same basic idea, incomprehensibly different scale.

## 3. "5% of the tasks"

This is a crucial detail. Emergence isn't happening for *every* task — just a fraction. But nobody can predict in advance *which* tasks will show emergent behavior at *which* scale. That unpredictability is what makes emergence both exciting and unsettling.

## 4. "the culmination of an internal, statistics-driven process"

This debate matters enormously. If emergence is "real" — if abilities genuinely appear from nowhere at scale — then we can't predict what future, larger models will be able to do. If it's a "mirage" — abilities building gradually but our measurements missing the early stages — then things are less mysterious but also less dramatic. Some researchers have since argued that changing how we measure performance makes the "sudden jumps" disappear, suggesting emergence may be partly an artifact of how we test.

## 5. "social bias emerges with enormous numbers of parameters"

This is the dark side of emergence: undesirable behaviors can also appear at scale, not just useful abilities. The same process that produces surprising competence can produce surprising harms. This is why "we can't predict what happens at scale" isn't just an intellectual curiosity — it has real consequences.
